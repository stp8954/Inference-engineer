# Visual strategy

Decision recorded 2026-08-22. Prompted by the question of whether to use
[manim](https://github.com/3b1b/manim) (3Blue1Brown's animation library) for the series.

## The anchor figure — "Where the bytes go"

**One recurring visual carries the series.** Introduced in Week 3 (once the KV cache exists and the
byte budget is complete), then referenced in nearly every post after it. Prototype:
`drafts/figures/fig4-anchor.*`, generated by `anchor.py`.

**What it shows:** the bytes moved for one decode step — model weights (16 GB for an 8B at BF16),
KV cache (0.54 GB at 4K context, 4.3 GB at 32K), activations — and the five families of attack on
that budget, each tagged with the week that covers it.

**Why the byte budget and not an attention diagram.** The intuitive anchor is a canonical
attention/transformer figure, since so many optimizations sound attention-flavored. That would be a
mistake: at decode, attention is *compute*-bound (AI ≈ N); the starvation is weight reloading. An
attention-centred anchor would reinforce, twenty times over, exactly the misconception Week 2 exists
to correct. The byte budget gets the anchoring benefit with the correct mental model — and it makes
the series legible as a campaign against a handful of terms rather than a bag of unrelated tricks.

**The five moves** (this taxonomy is the series' spine):
1. **Shrink the bytes** — quantization (W10–11), GQA/MLA (W13), sliding window & SSMs (W13, W19)
2. **Move fewer of them twice** — FlashAttention (W12)
3. **Pack them better** — PagedAttention (W6)
4. **Amortize the load** — continuous batching across users (W5), speculative decoding across tokens (W16)
5. **Split the workload** — disaggregated prefill/decode (W17)

**How it recurs.** `build(highlight=i)` dims every segment except the one a given post attacks, so
each week reuses the same image with its own target lit. Cheap after the first build, and it gives
readers instant orientation — they see the anchor and know where they are. Week 30's showdown can
show every attack landed at once.

**Sequencing note.** Weeks 1–2 lay the groundwork without it: Week 1's time-budget bar establishes
that memory movement dominates; Week 2's roofline establishes the two regimes. The anchor completes
the picture in Week 3 and is then permanent. Do not introduce it in Week 1 — the byte budget isn't
complete until the KV cache exists, and Week 1's power is its simplicity.

## The three tiers

| Tier | Tool | Cost per asset | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Static figures** (default) | hand-written SVG → 2× PNG (`drafts/figures/gen.py`) | ~30 min | Almost everything. Mechanisms, comparisons, budgets, charts. |
| **Interactive widgets** | single self-contained HTML file, sliders + canvas/SVG | 2–4 hrs | Anything with a parameter the reader should *play with* — batch size, context length, acceptance rate. |
| **Animation** | ManimCE | 4–10 hrs | Reserved. See shortlist below. |

## The rule

**Animate only where a static diagram genuinely fails**, i.e. where the *process evolving over
time* is the thing being taught. If a still image can carry it, use a still image — the series
has 30 deep dives, a weekly digest, KVScope, and a Rust engine to ship. Systematically animating
every post is the most plausible way this series dies around Week 6.

Budget: **4–6 animations across the whole 30 weeks.** Treat each one as a distribution bet, not a
comprehension aid — spend them on the posts that most need to travel.

## Animation shortlist (ranked)

1. **The KV cache filling / naive recomputation** — Week 3. *Strongest case in the series.* The
   redundancy of naive decode is invisible in a still and unmistakable in motion: same rows
   recomputed every step, then the cached version where only one row is new. If only one animation
   ever gets made, make this one.
2. **Continuous batching** — Week 5. Requests joining and leaving between forward passes, freed
   slots refilled immediately. Static diagrams of this are famously confusing; motion makes it
   trivial. Also the highest-leverage concept in the series, so it deserves the best asset.
3. **FlashAttention tiling + online softmax** — Week 12. Tiles moving HBM→SRAM, running max/sum
   updating, the N×N matrix never materializing. Literally 3b1b-shaped material.
4. **PagedAttention allocation** — Week 6. Blocks popped off the free list, scattered physically,
   block table resolving them; contiguous fragmentation vs paged packing side by side.
5. **Speculative decoding** — Week 16. Propose K, verify in one pass, reject at the first
   disagreement, resample from the residual. The accept/reject rule is much clearer animated.
6. **The roofline dot, recurring** — Week 2 onward. The same chart, the operating point moving
   right/up as each optimization lands. Could be the visual spine of the whole series: one scene,
   re-rendered per post with a new dot position. Cheapest per use because the scene is reused.

## Interactive widget candidates (cheaper, often better)

These beat video because the reader *drives* them, they work in the web version of Substack, and
they're a fraction of the build cost:

- **Roofline explorer** — sliders for batch size, context length, precision; dot moves, ridge point
  marked. Probably more valuable than the animated version above.
- **KV cache calculator** — model shape + context + batch → GB, and how many concurrent users fit
  on a chosen GPU. Directly useful, highly shareable.
- **Napkin calculator** — the Week 1 `napkin.py` logic as a web page: hardware + model + precision →
  predicted decode ceiling vs measured.
- **Acceptance-rate → speedup** — the geometric series from Week 16, as a curve you can drag.

Precedent: Vizuara shipped their "Inference Quest" companion as a single interactive HTML file
rather than a video series — same instinct, and their book is otherwise the closest analog to this
series.

## Practical notes

- **Use ManimCE**, the community edition — not 3b1b's `manimgl`. Grant's version is tuned for his own
  workflow and thinly documented; the community fork is stable and has answers findable online.
- **Video does not embed in Substack email** — subscribers get a thumbnail and a link. Animations are
  therefore primarily *web + X* assets. Plan them for posts you want to break out, and keep a static
  fallback figure in the email body.
- **Video posts get outsized reach on X**, which is the real argument for animation: a good KV-cache
  animation is the kind of asset other people embed in their own posts for years.
- Keep every generated asset in `drafts/figures/` with its source, same as the Week 1 figures — the
  figures are code, so when measured numbers replace predicted ones, re-run rather than redraw.

## Sequencing

Don't touch animation until the series has shipped ~4 posts and the cadence is proven. First
animation candidate: the KV cache, for Week 3 — but only if Weeks 1–3 are already drafted and the
buffer is intact. If the buffer is thin, ship the static figure and revisit.
